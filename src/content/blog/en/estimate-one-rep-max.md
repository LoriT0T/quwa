Testing a true one-rep max is expensive. It costs a session, it costs several days of recovery,
and it carries a small but real injury risk. Most of the time you do not need it — you need a
number good enough to programme percentages from.

## The three formulas

**Epley:** `1RM = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)`. Reads slightly high, especially at low rep counts.

**Brzycki:** `1RM = weight × 36 ÷ (37 − reps)`. Reads low as reps climb, and breaks down entirely
above about 12.

**Lombardi:** `1RM = weight × reps^0.10`. Behaves better at higher rep counts than the other two,
and worse at very low ones.

They disagree, sometimes by 5% or more. That disagreement is the useful information: it tells you
the honest uncertainty in the estimate.

## Use the average

Our [one-rep max calculator](/en/tools/one-rep-max) shows all three and averages them. The average
is more stable than any one formula across the rep ranges people actually train in, and it does
not require you to have a favourite.

If you need one number for programming, round the average **down** to the nearest 2.5 kg. An
overestimated max makes every percentage-based set in the block slightly too heavy, and that error
compounds across twelve weeks.

## Rep count is the accuracy variable

A set of 3 to 5 predicts a single well. A set of 15 does not — at that point you are measuring
muscular endurance, which correlates only loosely with a maximum single.

If you want a usable estimate, take a set of 5 or fewer to within one or two reps of failure. If
your set was 12+, treat the output as a rough bracket rather than a number.

## What the percentages are for

Once you have an estimate, the table gives you loads for a target rep range: about 86% for a
five-rep set, 78% for eight, 70% for twelve. These are averages across a population, and your
personal curve may be flatter or steeper — many lifters can do far more reps at 80% than the table
predicts, and a few can do far fewer.

Use the table to pick your first working weight. Then use what actually happened in the session to
pick the next one. The table is a starting point, not an instruction.
