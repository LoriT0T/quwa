The short answer is your maintenance calories plus roughly 10%. The longer answer is why that
number and not the 500-calorie surplus everyone repeats.

## Maintenance first, surplus second

You cannot set a surplus without knowing what you are adding it to. Maintenance is the intake
that holds your body weight steady over a fortnight, and every equation for estimating it —
including the one in our [TDEE calculator](/en/tools/tdee) — is a population average carrying
roughly ±10% of error when applied to one person.

So the estimate is the starting point, not the answer. Eat at the estimated figure for two weeks,
weigh yourself daily, and take the weekly average. If the average has not moved, that was your
maintenance. If it moved, you now know by how much and in which direction, which is better
information than any equation can give you.

## Why 10% and not 500 calories

A flat 500-calorie surplus means something completely different depending on your size. For a
55 kg person eating 1,900 kcal at maintenance, it is a 26% surplus. For a 95 kg person eating
3,100 kcal, it is 16%. The same number produces two very different rates of gain.

The rate matters because muscle accrual has a ceiling. Past a certain point, additional calories
do not go into more muscle — they go into more fat, and you have to diet that off later. Somewhere
around 10–15% above maintenance is where most people land: fast enough to support training, slow
enough that the ratio of muscle to fat stays reasonable.

## What a reasonable rate looks like

Roughly 0.25–0.5% of body weight per month is a sensible target for someone past their first year
of lifting. For an 80 kg lifter that is 200 to 400 grams a month. It looks unbearably slow on a
scale and it is genuinely fast in a mirror over twelve months.

If you are gaining considerably faster than that, the extra is very unlikely to be muscle.

## Protein, then the rest

Once calories are set, protein is the only macronutrient with a strong evidence-based target:
around 1.6–2.2 g per kilogram of body weight. Our [protein calculator](/en/tools/protein) gives
you the range and splits it across your meals.

Carbohydrate and fat after that are largely preference. Carbohydrate tends to make hard training
feel better, so if you train four or more times a week, biasing toward it is reasonable. Below
about 20% of calories from fat, some people report worse sleep and appetite regulation, so we set
that as a floor.

## When to change the number

Recalculate when your body weight has changed by more than about 5%, when your training volume
changes substantially, or when four weeks have passed with no movement on the scale.

Not weekly. A calorie target you keep adjusting is a target you never actually test.
